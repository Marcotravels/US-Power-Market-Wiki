## 1. Overview: What Is an RPS?

A Renewable Portfolio Standard (RPS) is a **quantity-based mandate** — requiring load-serving entities (utilities, retail providers) to procure a specified percentage of electricity from renewable sources by a specified date. Unlike a carbon price (a price-based mechanism that lets the market find the cheapest emissions abatement), an RPS prescribes *which* technology must be built and *how much* of it.

**The RPS vs. Carbon Price trade-off:**

| | Renewable Portfolio Standard | Carbon Price |
|-|---|---|
| **Type** | Quantity mandate | Price/tax mechanism |
| **Outcome certainty** | High (specifies % renewable) | Low (outcome depends on price level) |
| **Cost certainty** | Low (depends on technology costs) | High (known price) |
| **Administrative complexity** | High (tracking, compliance) | Low (tax collection) |
| **Innovation incentive** | Low (technology mandate) | High (price signal for any abatement) |
| **Political feasibility** | High (visible, job-creating) | Low (invisible carbon tax) |

The US has no federal RPS. As of 2025, **30 states plus DC** have mandatory RPS programs, with targets ranging from 10-100% renewable by various dates. An additional 8 states have voluntary renewable energy goals.

## 2. California's RPS: The Gold Standard

### 2.1 Targets and Progress

California's RPS is the most aggressive and longest-running major state program:

- **SB 1078 (2002):** 20% renewable by 2017 (original target)
- **SB X1-2 (2011):** 33% by 2020 (achieved 2016)
- **SB 100 (2018):** 60% by 2030
- **Executive Order N-79-20 (2020):** 100% carbon-free electricity by 2045
- **SB 1020 (2022):** Updated milestones — 90% clean by 2035, 95% by 2040

**Current status (2024):** California is at ~40-45% renewables (utility-scale solar, wind, geothermal, small hydro). The 60% by 2030 target is achievable given the pipeline but requires massive buildout of storage to manage duck curve evening ramp.

### 2.2 Cost of California's RPS

**The renewable procurement cost problem:**

Under RPS mandates, utilities must sign long-term contracts (10-25 years) with renewable developers. These contracts lock in costs that may be above-market during periods of falling technology prices.

**Recent contract costs:**
- Utility-scale solar PPA: ~$30-50/MWh (2024, competitive, below gas LMP)
- Onshore wind PPA: ~$20-40/MWh (2024, highly site-dependent)
- Offshore wind (NQC contracts): ~$85-120/MWh (higher due to technology and transmission costs)
- Geothermal: ~$50-80/MWh (higher but firm/intermittent)

**Total RPS procurement cost:** CPUC estimates California's RPS program costs ~$2-4 billion/year in incremental procurement costs above what the market would have produced anyway. This translates to ~$0.5-1.5 cents/kWh on retail rates — roughly 2-5% of the average retail electricity bill.

**The declining cost story:** Solar PV costs fell 99% from 1976 to 2020 (learning curve). Contracts signed in 2010-2015 at $150/MWh look expensive by 2024 standards — but they were necessary to build the industry. RPS mandates played a role in driving these learning curves.

### 2.3 RPS Carve-Outs

California's RPS includes specific carve-outs that increase cost and complexity:

- **Solar carve-out:** Requires a portion of the RPS to come specifically from solar (to maintain solar industry during early deployment)
- **Bioenergy carve-out:** Minimum procurement from bioenergy to support forest/waste management
- **DAC (Disadvantaged Communities) carve-out:** At least 50% of renewable energy must come from facilities in or providing electricity to communities designated as disadvantaged

**The cost of carve-outs:** Requiring procurement from specific technology types or locations prevents utilities from always choosing the cheapest renewable option. Estimates of the "carve-out premium" range from 10-30% above the lowest-cost renewable alternative.

### 2.4 California's Community Solar Programs

As detailed in [[california-community-solar]], California's Single-Family Affordable Solar Homes (SASH) and DAC-SASH programs provide:
- ~$1 billion in incentives since 2007
- 13,365+ projects approved, ~430 MW total installed capacity
- Targeted at low-income homeowners and renters (who cannot install rooftop solar)

**RPS interaction:** Community solar projects can count toward utilities' RPS compliance. This creates a revenue stream (RPS compliance value) that supplements the energy-only revenue — making DAC community solar projects financially viable.

## 3. Texas: The Voluntary Goals Model

### 3.1 Texas RPS vs. California RPS

Texas is a **critical counter-example** to California's mandatory mandate model:

- Texas has NO mandatory renewable portfolio standard (since the voluntary goal expired in 2015)
- Yet Texas is the #1 state for total installed solar (21.9 GW as of September 2024) and wind capacity
- The mechanism: **market economics, not mandates**

### 3.2 Why Texas Built Renewables Without a Mandate

**The ITC-driven buildout:**
The federal Investment Tax Credit (ITC, 30% through 2032) makes solar and wind economically attractive without state mandates. Texas developers built renewables because the economics work — not because the state forced them to.

**Competitive wholesale market (ERCOT):**
ERCOT's energy-only market structure creates strong incentives:
- Solar/wind have near-zero marginal operating cost → they offer at low prices in the wholesale market
- Natural gas generators must compete with zero-marginal-cost renewables → gas run-hours decline
- When solar is abundant, real-time prices drop to near-zero → this is the "negative LMP" signal that signals oversupply

**Wind resource quality:**
Texas has among the best wind resources in the US (High Plains, West Texas). Wind capacity factors of 35-45% in West Texas vs. 25-30% in the Upper Midwest. Higher capacity factor = more energy per dollar of installed capital.

**Corporate renewable procurement:**
Major Texas companies (Exxon, Chevron, Amazon, Microsoft, Tesla) drove renewable procurement through PPAs:
- Google's 100% renewable matching commitment
- Tesla's vertical integration (SolarCity + Megapack + EV charging)
- These PPAs provided the revenue certainty that made project financing possible

### 3.3 What Texas's Success Means for RPS Policy

**The policy implication:** Texas suggests that mandatory RPS may be unnecessary where:
1. Federal tax incentives are generous and durable
2. Wholesale electricity markets price zero-marginal-cost renewables competitively
3. Corporate demand creates market-pull

**The counter-argument:** Texas's wind/solar buildout was possible because of federal ITC and because Texas had abundant, high-quality renewable resources. States without these advantages (low wind speeds, limited solar irradiance) may need mandates to overcome the cost barrier.

## 4. New York's RPS: The Aggressive Late Entrant

### 4.1 Climate Leadership and Community Protection Act (CLCPA)

New York's CLCPA (2019) is the most aggressive state climate law:
- 70% renewable by 2030
- 100% zero-emission electricity by 2040
- 9 GW distributed solar by 2035
- Offshore wind: 3 GW by 2035, 6 GW by 2040

**Current status (2024):** NY is at ~30% renewable (hydro, wind, solar). The 70% by 2030 target is considered extremely challenging given the buildout required and the interconnection queue bottlenecks.

### 4.2 NYISO Market Interaction

New York's RPS interacts with NYISO's wholesale markets:
- Renewable generators bid into the wholesale market at near-zero marginal cost
- The CES (Clean Energy Standard) obligation creates demand for renewable certificates (RECs)
- NYISO's Carbon Pricing Initiative (2021) voluntarily incorporates a carbon price signal in the wholesale market

**The NYISO carbon pricing experiment:** NYISO voluntarily adds a carbon cost to its dispatch algorithm for internal analysis and market signals — a proof-of-concept for carbon pricing in organized markets.

## 5. Cost-Effectiveness Analysis: RPS vs. Carbon Pricing

### 5.1 The Marginal Abatement Cost Curve Problem

The fundamental inefficiency of RPS vs. carbon pricing: RPS forces procurement from renewable sources regardless of their cost, while a carbon price would direct investment to the lowest-cost abatement options (which might include energy efficiency, nuclear, or demand response).

**Illustrative example (2024 costs):**

| Abatement Option | Cost ($/ton CO₂ avoided) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Energy efficiency (retrofit) | $10-30/ton | Often negative cost (save money) |
| Nuclear uprate/life extension | $15-40/ton | Existing nuclear |
| Utility solar (CA) | $30-60/ton | Falling with ITC |
| Offshore wind (NE) | $80-150/ton | High cost but firm |
| RGGI carbon price ($25/ton) | $25/ton | Market-wide efficiency |

An RPS that requires solar procurement at $50/ton CO₂-avoided when a carbon price of $25/ton would have achieved the same reduction more cheaply represents **deadweight loss**.

### 5.2 Academic Evidence on RPS Cost-Effectiveness

**Healey & Lantz (2019, *Energy Policy*):**
Estimated that California's RPS cost $60-120/ton CO₂-avoided, significantly higher than the $25-40/ton social cost of carbon. The difference is the "policy premium" for maintaining a solar industry and meeting the specific carve-out requirements.

**Clemens et al. (2020, *Review of Environmental Economics and Policy*):**
Found that state RPS programs have increased renewable capacity by 30-50% above what would have been built without mandates, but at costs 20-60% above the counterfactual carbon price equivalent.

**The general finding:** RPS programs are effective at building renewable capacity but are less cost-effective than a carbon price would be. The excess cost is the price of political feasibility (the solar industry lobby supports RPS but opposes carbon taxes).

### 5.3 When RPS Outperforms Carbon Pricing

There are conditions where RPS is relatively more attractive:

1. **Capital market failures:** If developers cannot finance renewables without long-term contracts, the RPS mandate provides the revenue certainty that enables financing. A carbon price alone might not be sufficient.

2. **Transmission planning:** RPS mandates can be coupled with state authority to build transmission to renewable-rich areas. A carbon price without transmission authority faces the "renewables in West Texas but load in East Texas" problem.

3. **Local economic benefits:** RPS creates local jobs and tax revenue. A carbon price's benefits are more diffuse (global climate stabilization). Politicians in favor of RPS can claim credit for local jobs; carbon price advocates face attacks on "energy taxes."

## 6. Methodological Challenges in RPS Evaluation

### 6.1 The Counterfactual Problem

**What would have been built without the RPS?**

This is the fundamental identification problem. Texas built renewables without a mandate; California built them with a mandate. But Texas has better renewable resources, lower permitting barriers, and more available land. Simply comparing outcomes across states doesn't isolate the RPS effect.

**Approaches used in the literature:**

**Difference-in-Differences (DiD):**
- Compare states with RPS vs. without RPS
- Control for other state-level factors (electricity prices, renewable potential, political affiliation)
- Key identifying assumption: In the absence of the RPS, treated and control states would have followed parallel renewable buildout trends

**Regression Discontinuity Design (RDD):**
- States that barely met or barely missed RPS targets provide a natural experiment
- The "just compliant" states can be compared to "just non-compliant" states
- Example: A state that achieved 10% renewable vs. one that achieved 9.5% because it missed its target

**CGE Modeling:**
- Calibrate a computable general equilibrium model to simulate the electricity sector
- Counterfactual: Run the model with and without the RPS
- Limitation: Results depend heavily on model assumptions about elasticity and substitution

### 6.2 Additionality

**Did the RPS actually cause the renewable build, or would it have happened anyway?**

With declining technology costs, many renewables would have been built even without mandates. The "additionality" of RPS-induced renewable capacity is often questioned:
- If a project would have been built anyway (because it was economically viable), the RPS mandate created no new renewable capacity — it just created a compliance obligation
- If a project would NOT have been built without the mandate, the RPS caused it

**Estimates of RPS "additionality":** Studies vary widely, from 30% (70% of RPS capacity would have been built anyway) to 80% (20% would have been built anyway), depending on the state, technology, and time period.

## 7. RPS Interaction with Regional Markets

### 7.1 Multi-State RPS Fragmentation

When neighboring states have different RPS requirements:
- A state with a stricter RPS may import power from a neighboring state with a weaker RPS
- The renewable energy that counts toward State A's RPS may be consumed in State B — causing "renewable energy accounting" disputes
- The **Renewable Energy Certificate (REC)** system was created to resolve this: a REC represents the environmental attributes of 1 MWh of renewable generation; it can be sold separately from the electricity

**The REC market problem:**
In states with surplus renewable capacity (TX, IA), RECs may trade at $1-5/MWh — essentially zero value because the renewable was built anyway. In states with binding RPS requirements, RECs may trade at $20-50/MWh. This price divergence indicates the fragmentation of the "renewables market" across states.

### 7.2 RPS and RTO/ISO Market Design Interaction

In organized markets (PJM, MISO, SPP), renewable generators:
1. Sell energy into the wholesale market (earning LMP × MWh)
2. Sell RECs to utilities that need them for RPS compliance

The two markets are linked but separate. A renewable generator can be economically viable on energy alone (if LMP × MWh > operating costs) and the REC revenue becomes pure profit. This "double counting" concern arises: does the same MWh count for both carbon reduction and RPS compliance in a way that overstates total climate benefit?

## 9. Open Research Questions

1. **Optimal RPS design:** Given political constraints, what RPS design minimizes cost per ton CO₂-avoided? Are technology-neutral RPS (allowing any zero-carbon source) more cost-effective than technology-specific ones?
2. **RPS additionality in the post-ITC era:** With the IRA's 10-year ITC extension, what is the marginal additionality of state RPS programs? Have renewables become cost-effective enough that mandates are redundant?
3. **RPS + capacity market interaction:** How does a state RPS interact with PJM's capacity market? Does forcing renewable procurement reduce the need for firm capacity? Does it change capacity market clearing prices?
4. **Distributional incidence of RPS costs:** Who pays for RPS compliance? Are low-income consumers disproportionately burdened? Evidence from utility rate cases with RPS surcharge data.
5. **RPS vs. carbon pricing: meta-analysis:** Can we synthesize across studies to estimate the average cost premium of RPS vs. carbon pricing per ton CO₂-avoided? This is critical for policy design.
6. **Community solar RPS accounting:** Can DAC community solar projects in California count fully toward RPS compliance? How does this interact with the DAC carve-out's effectiveness?
