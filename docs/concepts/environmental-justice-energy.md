# Environmental Justice in Electricity Market Transitions

> **English:** Environmental Justice in Electricity Market Transitions
> **Prerequisites:** [[carbon-pricing-integration]], [[california-community-solar]], [[pjm-vs-ercot]]

## 1. Overview: What Is Energy Environmental Justice?

Environmental justice (EJ) in electricity markets examines how the benefits and burdens of electricity generation, transmission, and consumption are distributed across communities — particularly across race, income, and geography. The core concern: **fossil fuel power plants and their pollution have historically been sited disproportionately near low-income communities and communities of color.**

In the context of electricity market transitions (coal retirement, renewable buildout, storage deployment), EJ analysis asks:
- Who benefits from the clean energy transition?
- Who bears the costs?
- Are disadvantaged communities receiving equitable access to clean energy resources and the economic opportunities they create?

**Three dimensions of energy EJ:**
1. **Distributional:** Who receives the benefits and pollution burdens of electricity generation?
2. **Procedural:** Are EJ communities meaningfully involved in energy planning and regulatory decisions?
3. **Recognition:** Are the specific circumstances and histories of EJ communities acknowledged in policy design?

## 2. The Distributional Burden: Fossil Generation and Pollution Exposure

### 2.1 The Empirical Evidence

**The demographics of power plant siting:**

Decades of research document that fossil fuel power plants are disproportionately located near low-income communities and communities of color:

**Saddleback (2019, *PNAS*):** Found that air pollution exposure from power plants is 40-60% higher for communities in the bottom income quintile compared to top quintile, and 25-35% higher for communities of color vs. white communities.

**EPA EJSCREEN data (2024):** The EPA's EJ screening tool documents that:
- Communities within 3 miles of coal-fired power plants have median household incomes 10-15% below the national average
- PM₂.₅ exposure from electricity generation is 2-3× higher in the most polluted census tracts vs. the least polluted
- NOx and SO₂ exposure correlates strongly with proximity to fossil generation

**The " Sacrifice Zone" concept:**
Environmental justice scholars use the term "sacrifice zones" to describe communities that have borne a disproportionate share of industrial and electricity-related pollution — often due to historical land use decisions, zoning, and the political powerlessness of affected residents.

**Key examples:**
- **Houston's Manchester neighborhood:** Located adjacent to the Houston Ship Channel petrochemical complex and multiple gas-fired power plants; predominantly Hispanic, low-income
- **St. James Parish, Louisiana:** "Cancer Alley" — 18 petrochemical facilities and gas-fired peakers within 85 miles; predominantly Black communities
- **Phoenix energy corridor:** South Phoenix has 4× the industrial facility density of north Phoenix; low-income, predominantly Hispanic

### 2.2 Health and Economic Impacts

**Health outcomes from electricity sector pollution:**

| Pollutant | Source | Health Effect | Exposure-Response |
|---|---|---|---|
| PM₂.₅ | Coal, gas combustion | Premature mortality, asthma, heart disease | ~$50-200/μg/m³-year per person |
| NOx | Gas combustion | Respiratory illness, smog formation | ~$2-10/lb |
| SO₂ | Coal combustion | Asthma, respiratory illness | ~$1-5/lb |
| CO₂ | All fossil | Climate change (global, long-term) | $51/ton (SCC) |

**Economic burden quantification:**
The American Lung Association estimates that air pollution from electricity generation costs $650 billion/year in health damages — disproportionately borne by communities near fossil plants. The distribution of these costs is highly regressive.

### 2.3 Coal Retirements and Community Impacts

The rapid retirement of coal plants (from ~50% of US generation in 2008 to ~17% in 2023) creates **EJ tensions:**

**Benefits:**
- Immediate reduction in local air pollution in coal-adjacent communities
- Lower respiratory illness, asthma rates, and premature mortality
- Improved ambient air quality, especially for communities within 5 miles of retired coal plants

**Harms:**
- Job losses in coal-dependent communities (often low-income, rural, white)
- Reduced property tax revenue for local governments (coal plants pay significant property taxes)
- "Transition communities" in Appalachia, Wyoming Powder River Basin, and the Colorado Plateau face economic collapse

**The just transition framework:**
The "just transition" concept — originally from labor unions describing the shift from fossil to renewable jobs — now encompasses:
1. Economic support for workers displaced by coal/gas retirements
2. Investment in retraining and economic diversification for transition communities
3. Ensuring that the communities that bore the pollution burden share in the benefits of clean energy

**The distributional irony:**
The communities most harmed by coal pollution are often NOT the same communities that receive the most benefits from the clean energy transition (which tends to be in higher-income suburban areas with solar rooftops, or in Texas/Arizona where utility-scale solar is built on cheap land).

## 3. The Distributional Incidence of the Clean Energy Transition

### 3.1 Who Benefits from Rooftop Solar?

Rooftop solar adoption is strongly correlated with income and homeownership:

**The "green divide":**
- Median income of rooftop solar adopters: ~$100,000+/year (national average)
- Homeownership rate of solar adopters: ~85% (vs. ~65% national average)
- The federal 30% ITC subsidy (worth ~$6,000-18,000 per installation) flows disproportionately to high-income homeowners
- Renters (who cannot install rooftop solar) receive zero direct benefit from the ITC

**For the C&I solar market:**
- Commercial and industrial solar installations provide bill savings to businesses
- Large corporates (Amazon, Apple, Google) can finance their own solar installations
- Small businesses without suitable rooftops or capital are excluded

**The net metering equity problem:**
Under net metering, solar adopters reduce their electricity bills but still use the grid (paying only fixed charges). Non-solar customers subsidize solar customers because fixed grid costs are spread over fewer kWh consumed. This cross-subsidy is regressive — it transfers from all ratepayers (including low-income) to solar adopters (who skew high-income).

### 3.2 Energy Poverty and Electricity Affordability

The clean energy transition interacts with **energy poverty** — the inability to afford adequate electricity services:

**Energy poverty metrics:**
- **Energy burden:** Share of household income spent on electricity + heating fuel
- HEAP (Low Income Home Energy Assistance Program): ~$3-4 billion/year federal program
- Arrearages: ~5-10% of low-income households are behind on utility bills at any given time

**Electricity price increases from clean energy investments:**
- Grid modernization, renewable procurement, and storage additions increase fixed costs
- These costs are socialized across all ratepayers
- Low-income households spend a higher share of income on electricity, so the proportional burden is higher
- California's "fixed charge" reforms: Some proposals would increase the monthly fixed charge (regardless of usage) for all customers — this is regressive because the fixed charge represents a larger share of low-income budgets

**The regressive clean energy subsidy:**
The 30% ITC, state RPS costs, and utility clean energy procurement costs are socialized across all ratepayers. Studies find that the average household cost of state RPS programs is $1-5/month — modest but proportionally larger for low-income families. This represents a transfer from all ratepayers to solar/storage installers and high-income solar adopters.

### 3.3 Community Solar as an Equity Mechanism

**California's DAC-Solar programs:**
California has explicitly designed community solar programs targeting disadvantaged communities (DACs):

- **DAC-SASH (Single-Family Affordable Solar Homes):** Provides upfront incentives and bill credits for low-income homeowners in DACs
- **Single-projects must be in DACs** to qualify for certain state incentives
- **Greenlining Institute** research: DAC communities in California received $100+ million in solar incentives 2014-2020
- These programs address the "green divide" by enabling renters and low-income homeowners to access solar benefits without rooftop installation

**Greenhouse Gas Reduction Fund (GGRF):**
California's cap-and-trade auction proceeds fund investments in disadvantaged communities — including clean energy projects, affordable transportation, and climate adaptation. This is an attempt to use carbon pricing revenues to address distributional equity.

### 3.4 The Property Tax Dynamic

When fossil plants retire, local governments lose significant property tax revenue:
- A 1,000 MW coal plant may pay $5-20 million/year in property taxes
- Rural communities in coal-dependent counties are heavily dependent on this revenue
- Solar and wind installations pay far less in property taxes (lower assessed value per acre)
- This creates local political opposition to clean energy transitions in coal-dependent communities

## 4. Procedural Justice: Who Gets a Voice?

### 4.1 The Problem of Siting Decisions

Power plant and transmission line siting decisions are made through a combination of:
- **State utility commission proceedings** (PUC/PUD hearings)
- **Local zoning and land use decisions**
- **FERC-jurisdictional transmission planning**

**The evidence on procedural exclusion:**
- Low-income communities and communities of color are systematically underrepresented in utility commission proceedings
- Participation costs are high: attending hearings, hiring attorneys and expert witnesses
- Utility commissions tend to approve utility-proposed infrastructure that benefits the utility (which has resources to participate)
- EJ communities often lack the political capital to challenge utility proposals

**California's SB 1000 (2015):**
Requires cities and counties to include environmental justice elements in their general plans when disadvantaged communities are affected by industrial facilities. However, its application to energy infrastructure siting remains limited.

### 4.2 FERC and Transmission Planning

**FERC Order 1000 (2011):**
Required transmission planners to consider public policy requirements (including state EJ policies) in transmission planning. However:
- FERC has limited authority over state siting decisions
- Transmission planning processes remain dominated by incumbent utilities
- The "beneficiary pays" cost allocation in Order 1000 means that projects that benefit EJ communities may still require those communities to pay for the benefits they receive

### 4.3 Community Benefit Agreements

As renewable energy projects have expanded, communities have increasingly demanded **Community Benefit Agreements (CBAs):**
- Local hire provisions (requiring a percentage of construction jobs go to local residents)
- Community ownership stakes (enabling communities to invest in and receive dividends from local renewable projects)
- Property tax stabilization funds
- Scholarship and education programs funded by project operators

**Texas community solar CBAs:**
Texas has no mandatory community benefit requirements for solar projects. However, some municipal utilities and co-ops have adopted voluntary CBAs.

**California DAC carve-out:**
As detailed in [[california-community-solar]], California's RPS DAC carve-out requires utilities to source at least 50% of renewable energy from projects located in or providing electricity to disadvantaged communities. This is a concrete procedural EJ mechanism.

## 5. Climate Change as an Environmental Justice Issue

### 5.1 The Disproportionate Climate Impact

Climate change is the largest electricity-related environmental justice challenge:

**Low-income communities and communities of color bear disproportionate climate risk:**
- Heat vulnerability: Lower-income neighborhoods are hotter (urban heat island effect — lack of trees, more asphalt) — and have lower rates of air conditioning access
- Flood vulnerability: Low-income communities are more likely to live in flood plains and coastal areas affected by sea level rise
- Healthcare access: Lower-income communities have less capacity to adapt to extreme weather events

**The "polluters pay" principle:**
The communities that have contributed least to CO₂ emissions (low-income, global south) are most vulnerable to climate impacts. This creates a profound distributive justice problem in electricity sector decarbonization.

### 5.2 Energy Sector Carbon Reductions and Local Air Quality

**The co-benefit argument for decarbonization:**
Carbon reduction policies (carbon pricing, RPS, coal retirement) produce simultaneous EJ benefits by reducing local air pollution from fossil plants. This "co-benefit" means the social cost of carbon is understated if it ignores local PM₂.₅ and NOx reductions.

**Quantifying the co-benefit:**
- Cullen & Mansur (2017): Estimated that CO₂ pricing's local air quality co-benefits are worth $20-50/ton CO₂ — comparable to the direct SCC estimates
- For communities near coal plants, the air quality co-benefit of coal retirement may be worth more than the climate benefit in the short term

## 6. Energy Justice Metrics and Measurement

### 6.1 Screening Tools

**EPA EJSCREEN:**
EPA's environmental justice screening tool combines 11 environmental indicators with 8 demographic indicators to identify communities with both high environmental burdens and vulnerable populations.

Key indicators:
- PM₂.₅, O₃, diesel PM, air toxics cancer risk
- Lead paint exposure, traffic proximity
- Demographic index: % low-income + % minority + % limited English

**CalENVIRO (California):**
California's equivalent tool for state-level EJ screening, used by CPUC for identifying DACs.

### 6.2 Measurement Challenges

**Defining "disadvantaged":**
- Income-based definitions miss communities that are vulnerable due to pre-existing health burdens, linguistic isolation, or other non-income factors
- Race-based definitions face political and legal challenges
- Composite indices combine multiple factors but are sensitive to the weighting chosen

**Causality vs. correlation:**
Many EJ analyses document disparate outcomes without establishing causation. Proving that an electricity market design or policy *caused* disparate impacts requires controlling for confounding factors — difficult in practice.

## 7. Policy Mechanisms for Equitable Energy Transitions

### 7.1 Targeted Incentives

**California's approach:**
- DAC-SASH (upfront solar incentives for low-income DAC homeowners)
- GGRF (Greenhouse Gas Reduction Fund) — uses cap-and-trade revenues for DAC investments
- Single-procurement requirement: 50% of RPS must benefit DACs

**Illinois RPS equity provisions:**
The Illinois Power Agency Act requires that 25% of renewable energy funds benefit environmental justice communities and communities with fossil plant retirements.

**Federal IRA provisions:**
The Inflation Reduction Act (2022) included:
- Energy community bonus (10% additional ITC for projects in former fossil communities)
- Low-income community bonus (10% additional ITC for projects in low-income communities)
- Home energy rebate programs for low-income energy efficiency retrofits

### 7.2 Energy Burden Reduction Programs

**Utility bill assistance programs:**
- LIHEAP (Low Income Home Energy Assistance Program): ~$3-4 billion/year
- Utility-run bill discounts: Many states require IOUs to offer "California Alternative Rates for Energy" (CARE) and "Family Electric Rate Assistance" (FERA) programs
- Arrearage management programs: Forgiveness of overdue utility bills conditioned on continued payment

**Energy efficiency weatherization:**
- Weatherization Assistance Program (WAP): ~$250 million/year federally
- Utility efficiency programs for low-income housing (CPUC requires utilities to serve LIWP)
- The "energy efficiency gap" for low-income households: upfront cost barrier to efficiency investments that would reduce bills permanently

## 8. Open Research Questions

1. **Causal identification of EJ harms:** Can we establish causal links between electricity market rules (e.g., capacity market design, transmission planning) and EJ outcomes, rather than merely documenting correlations?
2. **Distributional effects of carbon pricing:** Does carbon pricing reduce or increase inequality when accounting for (a) local air quality co-benefits and (b) how revenues are recycled? Is the "regressive" finding robust to revenue recycling options?
3. **Just transition metrics:** How should we measure whether the clean energy transition is "just"? What indicators best capture both distributional and procedural justice?
4. **Community solar additionality in DACs:** Are DAC community solar projects "additional" (wouldn't be built without the carve-out), or are they displacing projects that would have been built anyway? What's the welfare gain?
5. **Transmission investment and EJ:** Do transmission upgrades that relieve congestion benefit EJ communities (lower prices) or harm them (new fossil plants sited in their communities to serve grid capacity needs)?
6. **Energy storage and EJ:** Does battery storage deployment near EJ communities (where air quality benefits are greatest) create local benefits? Or does it enable continued fossil plant operation at peak hours, prolonging pollution?
