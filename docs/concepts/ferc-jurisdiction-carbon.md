# FERC Jurisdiction Over Carbon Pricing in Electricity Markets

> **English:** FERC Jurisdiction and Carbon Pricing in RTO/ISO Markets
> **Prerequisites:** [[carbon-pricing-integration]], [[renewable-interconnection-policy]]

---

## 1. Overview: The Federalism Problem

The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) regulates wholesale electricity markets under the Federal Power Act (FPA), while states retain authority over retail electricity rates, resource planning, and many environmental policies. Carbon pricing sits at the intersection of these jurisdictions — creating a fundamental legal and economic tension.

**The core question:** Can FERC mandate carbon pricing in wholesale electricity markets operated by RTOs/ISOs, or does that authority belong to states?

**The practical stakes:** If states cannot agree on a federal carbon price and FERC cannot impose one, then carbon pricing in US electricity markets will remain a patchwork of fragmented state programs — with all the leakage and competitive distortion that implies.

---

## 2. FERC's Legal Authority Under the Federal Power Act

### 2.1 FERC's Jurisdictional Scope

**Under the Federal Power Act (FPA), FERC has jurisdiction over:**
- Wholesale electricity sales and transmission in interstate commerce
- Rates, terms, and conditions of service in RTO/ISO markets
- Market rules for FERC-approved RTOs and ISOs
- Natural gas pipeline transportation and pricing (Section 1)
- Hydroelectric licensing (Section 4)

**States retain authority over:**
- Retail electricity rates (the "retail rate" exception)
- Generation siting (under the "Coppari" doctrine)
- Resource adequacy standards
- Environmental regulations of generation facilities (Title I of the Clean Air Act delegation)

### 2.2 FERC's Jurisdiction Over RTO/ISO Markets

Since FERC Order 2000 (2000), FERC has approved and regulated RTOs/ISOs as Independent System Operators that administer wholesale markets under FERC oversight.

**FERC's authority over RTO/ISO market design includes:**
- Approving market rules (LMP, capacity auctions, ancillary services)
- Setting rate caps and price formation mechanisms
- Ordering reliability coordinator functions
- Approving interregional coordination mechanisms

**But FERC's authority does NOT extend to:**
- Setting retail electricity rates
- Directly regulating generation emissions (EPA authority under Clean Air Act)
- Mandating how states should price carbon

### 2.3 The Carbon Pricing Gap

There is currently **no federal carbon price** for electricity. FERC has never approved a carbon pricing mechanism in any RTO/ISO market. The EPA's Clean Power Plan (2015, struck down) and its successor rules have attempted emissions reductions through technology standards — not market-based carbon pricing.

**Why hasn't FERC acted on carbon pricing?**
- FERC operates under the "just and reasonable" standard — it can only impose rates/market rules that benefit consumers
- A carbon price is fundamentally a *policy* choice made by Congress and/or states, not a market efficiency improvement
- FERC would need explicit congressional authorization to impose a carbon charge in RTO/ISO markets

## 3. Can FERC Mandate Carbon Pricing?

### 3.1 The Affirmative Case

**Market efficiency argument:**
- Without carbon pricing, wholesale electricity markets systematically ignore the external cost of CO₂ → inefficient dispatch
- Just as FERC has required RTOs to price transmission congestion (LMP), it could theoretically require them to price carbon as an input to dispatch efficiency
- The analogy: FERC already accounts for generation costs, fuel costs, and emissions costs through market mechanisms — adding a carbon cost is an extension, not a revolution

**The "indirect" mechanism:**
Rather than directly mandating a carbon price, FERC could:
1. Require RTOs to incorporate the **Social Cost of Carbon (SCC)** in dispatch algorithms as a proxy for unpriced externality
2. Order RTOs to modify offer caps to reflect carbon costs for imports from non-carbon-priced regions
3. Mandate "carbon accounting" in capacity market accreditation — resources in non-carbon-priced states get lower capacity credit when serving load in carbon-priced states

**The New York ISO (NYISO) precedent:**
NYISO has operated a "Carbon Pricing" initiative since 2021 — voluntarily incorporating a carbon price signal into its wholesale market clearing. This was done through NYISO's own market design process, not FERC mandate. It provides a proof-of-concept that carbon pricing can work within FERC-approved market rules.

### 3.2 The Negative Case

**Legal barriers:**
1. **State police powers:** States have broad authority over environmental policy under the 10th Amendment. A federal agency imposing a carbon tax on state utilities would face Dormant Commerce Clause and federalism challenges.

2. **Major questions doctrine:** Under West Virginia v. EPA (2022), federal agencies cannot make decisions of vast economic and political significance without clear congressional authorization. A FERC carbon pricing mandate would almost certainly trigger this doctrine.

3. **Retained state authority:** FERC's own precedent (from Order 719 and earlier) recognizes that states retain authority over resource adequacy and retail rate design. Carbon pricing is arguably a state prerogative.

**Political economy:**
- FERC operates by majority vote of 5 commissioners (currently 3D-2R as of 2025)
- Carbon pricing is politically contested — a FERC mandate without congressional action would face immediate legal challenge and political backlash
- FERC has historically avoided policy decisions that should be made by Congress

### 3.3 The Legislative Path

The cleanest legal path to federal carbon pricing in electricity markets:

1. **Congress passes a carbon price** (e.g., a carbon tax or cap-and-trade bill)
2. **EPA/FERC implement** under explicit statutory authority
3. **Carbon price flows through** to wholesale electricity markets automatically

This path failed repeatedly (2009 Waxman-Markey, 2010, various others) and shows no signs of near-term prospects.

## 4. State Carbon Border Adjustments and the Dormant Commerce Clause

### 4.1 The California Import Compliance Mechanism

As described in [[carbon-pricing-integration]], California's AB 398 (2017) extended the cap-and-trade program to cover electricity imports. This requires California importers to surrender carbon allowances for electricity generated outside California that is consumed in California.

**The DCC challenge:**
- *Energy @and Natural* Gas Corp. v. California Public Utilities Commission (litigation ongoing)
- Arguments: The mechanism discriminates against out-of-state generators (in-state generators receive free allocation; importers don't), it regulates extraterritorial emissions, and it imposes a burden on interstate commerce that exceeds any in-state benefit

### 4.2 The RGGI Import Gap

RGGI states explicitly do NOT include electricity imports in their compliance mechanism — precisely because of the DCC concerns that California is now testing in litigation.

**The consequence:** A coal plant in West Virginia selling power to New York faces no RGGI carbon cost. But an in-state RGGI gas plant does. This creates:
- Competitive disadvantage for RGGI gas generators vs. West Virginia coal
- Pressure on RGGI states to import more cheap coal-fired power from outside the region (leakage)

**The RGGI Board has studied import mechanisms** and concluded that without California-like import compliance, the leakage problem is substantial. Estimates: 10-30% of in-state emission reductions from RGGI are offset by increased imports from neighboring states.

### 4.3 Constitutional Analysis

**The Pike v. Bruce Church balancing test (1970):**
When a state law affects interstate commerce, courts balance the local benefit against the burden on interstate commerce.

**For CA import compliance:**
- Local benefit: Reduced CO₂ from CA electricity consumption (including imported electricity)
- Burden: Distortion of competitive wholesale electricity markets, discrimination against out-of-state generators
- Outcome: Uncertain — depends on how courts weight climate benefit vs. economic burden

**The extraterritoriality problem:**
The most serious constitutional flaw: CA is effectively trying to regulate emissions that occur in West Virginia, Kentucky, etc. Under the *Maine v. Taylor* (1986) and *Healy v. Beer Institute* (1989) precedents, states cannot regulate conduct outside their borders.

**Counter-argument:** CA is not regulating emissions — it is taxing the *consumption* of electricity within California, which is a legitimate state police power. The compliance obligation falls on the CA importer, not the out-of-state generator.

## 5. Federal Preemption of State Climate Policies

### 5.1 FERC Preemption Doctrine

Under Supremacy Clause doctrine, federal regulations can preempt state regulations when:
1. Congress has "occupied the field" (field preemption)
2. Federal and state law are in "actual conflict" (conflict preemption)
3. State law "stands as an obstacle" to federal objectives (obstacle preemption)

**FERC's field preemption argument:**
If FERC has approved a specific market design for an RTO/ISO, do state environmental policies that affect wholesale market outcomes conflict with that design?

**Key case: *Northeast Power Coordinating Council v. FERC* (2006):**
FERC has authority over reliability standards — but states retain authority over resource mix. This case established that state preferences for certain generation types (e.g., renewable portfolio standards) are NOT preempted by FERC's wholesale market authority.

### 5.2 The Carbon Border Adjustment Parallel

The EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) provides a useful reference:
- The EU imposes a carbon price on imported goods from countries without equivalent carbon pricing
- The US is studying similar mechanisms at the federal level
- State-level CBAM equivalents (like CA's) face DCC challenges

**Theoretical framework:** A properly designed carbon border adjustment for electricity:
- Is uniform in its application (doesn't discriminate between states)
- Is based on actual emissions intensity (not arbitrary discrimination)
- Provides credit for carbon prices already paid in the exporting jurisdiction

This is the framework that would survive DCC scrutiny — and the one that would require federal-level implementation to achieve uniformity across all US states.

## 6. The Compliance Carbon Cost Pass-Through Framework

### 6.1 How Carbon Costs Flow in RTO/ISO Markets

When a generator has a carbon compliance cost (from CA cap-and-trade or RGGI), how does that affect the market clearing?

**In a bilateral market:** Generator and utility negotiate a contract; carbon cost is explicitly included in the offer price.

**In an RTO/ISO market (PJM, CAISO):** The generator submits an offer to the market. If the generator's carbon cost is higher than its marginal cost bid, it will be less competitive in the dispatch stack. The market clears at the price that balances supply and demand.

**The pass-through question:** How much of the carbon compliance cost gets embedded in the offer price vs. absorbed by the generator?

- If generators are competitive price-takers: Carbon cost is passed through proportionally to LMP
- If generators have market power: They may already be setting prices above marginal cost — the carbon cost may be absorbed by reducing markups rather than raising prices

### 6.2 Forward Contract Interactions

In organized markets, generators typically hedge 70-90% of their output through forward contracts. The carbon compliance cost is factored into these contracts at the time of signing. This means:

1. **Short-term spot market:** Pass-through of carbon cost is more direct (spot prices reflect current marginal cost including carbon)
2. **Forward contract market:** Carbon costs are "baked in" to forward prices — consumers who buy long-term contracts pay the carbon cost without seeing it as a separate line item
3. **Capacity market:** Capacity payments are not directly affected by carbon costs unless the carbon price affects the expected energy market revenue (which feeds into capacity adequacy calculations)

## 7. Federal Carbon Pricing Scenarios

### 7.1 Scenario 1: Federal Carbon Tax

A federal carbon tax of $50/ton applied at the point of fossil fuel production (mine mouth, wellhead) would:
- Automatically flow through to electricity prices as a fuel cost increase
- Preempt state carbon pricing programs (state programs might be credited against federal tax)
- Be administratively simple (IRS collection)
- Face massive political opposition

**Electricity market impact:** A $50/ton carbon tax on natural gas (0.053 t CO₂/MMBtu) adds ~$2.65/MMBtu. At a gas heat rate of 7,000 BTU/kWh, this adds ~$18.5/MWh to gas marginal cost. For coal (0.093 t CO₂/MMBtu, 10,000 BTU/kWh), adds ~$46.5/MWh.

### 7.2 Scenario 2: Federal Cap-and-Trade

Federal cap-and-trade covering electricity + industrial sectors would:
- Link with existing state programs (CA, RGGI) — potentially providing allowance supply that reduces federal market prices
- Create a national carbon price that eliminates interstate competitiveness concerns
- Face the same political challenges as a carbon tax

**Market design interaction:** FERC would need to approve modifications to RTO/ISO market rules to incorporate federal allowance prices in dispatch algorithms.

### 7.3 Scenario 3: FERC Carbon Pricing Rule

A FERC rule requiring RTOs/ISOs to incorporate a "shadow carbon price" in market dispatch:

- **Not a compliance carbon price** — just an accounting cost used in market clearing
- Could use the EPA's Social Cost of Carbon ($51/ton 2023 central estimate)
- Would shift dispatch toward lower-carbon resources without mandating emissions caps
- **Legal challenge:** Likely exceeds FERC's statutory authority without Congressional authorization

## 8. Open Research Questions

1. **Optimal federal carbon pricing instrument:** Given political constraints, what is the most feasible federal carbon pricing mechanism for electricity? Carbon tax vs. cap-and-trade vs. clean electricity standard?
2. **DCC analysis of state carbon import mechanisms:** Can states constitutionally apply carbon prices to imported electricity? What are the precise constitutional boundaries?
3. **Market power + carbon pricing interaction:** When generators have market power in constrained transmission zones, does a carbon price get passed through to consumers, or do generators absorb it as rent? How does this affect welfare?
4. **Preemption of state programs:** If Congress authorizes a federal carbon price, what happens to CA cap-and-trade and RGGI? Are they preempted, credited, or allowed to continue?
5. **Pass-through with capacity markets:** How does the presence of a capacity market (PJM, NYISO) affect the pass-through of carbon costs to wholesale electricity prices compared to energy-only markets (ERCOT)?
6. **International competitiveness effects:** Do carbon pricing programs (or their absence) affect industrial location decisions? Evidence from CA vs. TX differential would be highly informative.
